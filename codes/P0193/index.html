P0193

Fuel Rail Pressure Sensor Circuit High

Severity
High
Driving
Drive only to a garage
System
Powertrain
Group
Fuel and air metering
Important. A high pressure fuel system is dangerous to work on. A common rail diesel runs well over 1,500 bar and a direct injection petrol several hundred, and the rail stays pressurised after the engine is switched off. Fuel escaping from a pinhole at those pressures passes straight through skin and injects into the tissue underneath: it can look like a trivial puncture and it is a surgical emergency. Never run a hand along a high pressure line to feel for a leak, never slacken a union with the engine running, and always follow the manufacturer's depressurisation procedure before touching the high pressure side.

The fuel rail pressure signal is too high - outside the range the sensor is built to produce, which points at the circuit rather than at the fuel pressure itself. A lost 5 volt supply or earth drives the signal hard to one end, and that is a wiring fault rather than a failed sensor.

Possible causes

  • CommonFailed fuel rail pressure sensor
  • CommonCorroded or damaged connector at the sensor
  • CommonWiring fault between the sensor and the module
  • CommonGenuine fuel supply problem - blocked filter, weak pump, air in the system - that the sensor is correctly reporting
  • PossibleFuel pressure regulator or metering valve fault
  • PossibleWorn high pressure pump

What you might notice

  • Hard starting, or refusing to start at all
  • Loss of power, or dropping into limp mode
  • Rough running, misfiring, or cutting out
  • Smoke from the exhaust
  • Check engine light on

What to check, in order

  1. Read all codes before deciding this is the sensor. Fuel delivery and pressure regulation codes stored alongside usually mean the sensor is telling the truth about a supply problem, and replacing it would fix nothing.
  2. Compare commanded rail pressure against actual on live data, at idle and under load. A system that holds pressure at idle and loses it under load is worn rather than broken.
  3. Check the low pressure supply and the filter first. Most rail pressure faults start upstream, and the filter is the cheapest thing in the chain.
  4. Inspect the sensor connector for corrosion and for damaged pins.
  5. Check the sensor's supply and earth at the connector before replacing anything. This is a five minute test that regularly saves an expensive part.
